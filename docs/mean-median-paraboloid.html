Mean, median and centroid of a paraboloid

A horizontal plane cutting a homogeneous solid into equal volumes need not pass through its centroid. The centroid is a mean; the volume-bisecting plane is a median. These two statistical functionals generally differ.

Sectional-area model

Let a solid of height H > 0 have nonnegative horizontal cross-sectional area A(z) for 0 ≤ z ≤ H. Assume uniform mass density and positive total volume V = ∫0H A(z) dz. The height Z of a uniformly sampled point in the solid has probability density fZ(z) = A(z)/V.

The centroid height is the mean z̄ = (1/V) ∫0H zA(z) dz. A volume-bisecting plane is located at height m satisfying ∫0m A(z) dz = V/2. Thus, z̄ and m are respectively the mean and a median of Z.

Exact result for power-law sections

Suppose A(z) = Czp, with C > 0 and p ≥ 0. Then V = CHp+1/(p+1). For U = Z/H, its cumulative distribution is FU(u) = up+1, 0 ≤ u ≤ 1.

Direct integration yields the normalized centroid and median heights:

z̄/H = (p + 1)/(p + 2)    and    m/H = 2−1/(p + 1).

The fraction of volume below the centroid plane is FU(z̄/H) = [(p+1)/(p+2)]p+1, which need not equal 1/2.

A paraboloid of revolution

Consider a paraboloid with its vertex at z = 0 and upper plane at z = H. Since the squared radius of a horizontal section is proportional to z, its sectional area is proportional to z: this is the case p = 1.

Centroid: z̄ = 2H/3    Volume bisector: m = H/√2.

Consequently, the volume below the centroid is (2/3)2 = 4/9 of the total volume, not one half. The volume-bisecting plane lies above the centroid: m − z̄ = H(1/√2 − 2/3) ≈ 0.04044H.

The mean balances the first moment of volume around a horizontal plane; the median divides volume into equal halves. Equal volume is not equivalent to equal moment.

Comparison with other solids

For a cylinder (p = 0), the mean and median coincide at H/2. For a cone with vertex at z = 0 (p = 2), the centroid is at 3H/4, while the volume bisector is at 2−1/3H. The height distribution, rather than the visual midpoint, determines each plane.

The power-law density remains meaningful for p > 2, but such a profile cannot arise from a three-dimensional convex solid. The interactive laboratory also examines frustums and more general sectional-area functions.
